      title: Institutional ethnography of nursing and healthcare
      text: |
        Institutional ethnography starts with people’s everyday work and follows the relations that organise it. In healthcare, that can mean tracing how a policy, staffing decision or electronic record connects a difficult shift to decisions made elsewhere.

        ## Where the method comes from

        Dorothy E. Smith developed institutional ethnography within feminist sociology. Its concern with **ruling relations** directs attention to how institutions coordinate people’s activities through texts and practices. Her [*Institutional Ethnography: A Sociology for People* (2005)](https://www.bloomsbury.com/uk/institutional-ethnography-9780759105027/) is a starting point for the approach.

        Carol A. Taylor and Nicola Fairchild’s [work on posthumanist institutional ethnography (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1080/17457823.2020.1735469) brought this inquiry into conversation with feminist materialist and posthuman approaches in education. My work develops that conversation in nursing; it does not originate institutional ethnography or posthumanist institutional ethnography.

        ## My contribution in nursing

        My Edinburgh doctoral thesis, [*Ecologies of Care* (2023)](https://era.ed.ac.uk/items/351c3676-c09d-42e0-b8a1-98a09eb19475), studied nursing in an acute NHS hospital. It combined workforce demographic data, analysis of the Nursing and Midwifery Council Code, participatory ethnography, multimedia diaries and interviews.

        With Rosie Stenhouse, I examined the tensions between individual professional accountability and the conditions of nursing practice in [*Navigating the System of Regulation and Practice in the UK*](https://doi.org/10.1111/jan.17077).

        This work attends to the participation of bodies, technologies, spaces and material conditions in care while following nurses’ accounts of their work. [Read more about the critical posthuman perspectives behind it →](/critical-posthumanism/)

        ## What an inquiry can follow

        A starting question might be: *what happens when a nurse cannot carry out the care that a standard requires?* Interviews and observation can establish what people actually do. Following the records, policies and decisions involved can then show how that work is coordinated beyond the ward.

        The methods depend on the question. My studies have used observation, interviews, diaries, documentary analysis and workforce data. The aim is to explain connections and contradictions in the organisation of work, rather than to treat local experience as a self-contained problem.

        ## Working together

        I welcome collaborations on nursing work, professional regulation, workforce wellbeing and healthcare technologies. Institutional ethnographic inquiry can be useful where a problem is experienced locally but organised across institutions.
